From questions to credible evidence.

Research requires more than finding information. My workflow emphasizes tracing claims to their original sources, understanding how evidence was generated, evaluating its limitations, and organizing findings for further scholarly inquiry.

  1. 01

    Define

    Clarify the research question, relevant population, variables, and the type of evidence needed.

    Identify whether the question requires historical evidence, academic literature, demographic statistics, or survey-based findings.

  2. 02

    Discover

    Identify relevant peer-reviewed publications, historical research, and credible survey reports.

    Use academic search platforms, university library resources, and original research publications.

  3. 03

    Verify

    Cross-check preliminary claims against original publications.

    Verify:

    • Numerical estimates
    • Reported percentages
    • Original quotations
    • Publication details
    • Page references
    • Sample characteristics
    • Measurement definitions

    Do not treat AI-generated summaries or secondary descriptions as substitutes for original evidence.

  4. 04

    Evaluate

    Examine how the evidence was generated.

    Consider:

    • Research methodology
    • Sample composition
    • Historical period
    • Population coverage
    • Variable definitions
    • Measurement scales
    • Potential sources of bias
  5. 05

    Synthesize

    Organize findings into structured research documentation.

    Connect each research claim with its supporting evidence, original source, sample context, interpretation, and limitations.

    Distinguish verified findings from evidence that requires further investigation.

  6. 06

    Communicate

    Present research findings in a clear, traceable, and academically useful format.

    Communicate both the strengths and limitations of available evidence to support further faculty review.

A statistic is only as strong as its context.

When reviewing empirical research, I consider not only the reported result but also the evidence underlying it. Understanding the source, sample, measurement, historical context, and limitations is essential to determining what a finding can reasonably support.

Source

Where did the evidence originate?

Is the claim supported by an original academic publication, a historical dataset, or a survey report?

Can the original finding be independently traced and verified?

Sample

Who was studied?

What population does the sample represent?

Are the sample characteristics appropriate for the research question?

Measurement

How were the relevant variables defined?

Do different studies measure the same construct?

Are reported statistics based on comparable scales and definitions?

Context

When was the evidence collected?

What historical, demographic, or institutional conditions might influence its interpretation?

Can findings from one cohort reasonably inform questions about another?

Limitations

What assumptions or biases affect the evidence?

Are there limitations associated with self-reporting, sample selection, measurement, or generalizability?

What additional evidence would be needed to support a stronger conclusion?

What the evidence can—and cannot—tell us.

Historical Context Matters

Contemporary findings may not accurately represent populations from several decades earlier.

Historical changes in educational policies, demographic composition, and institutional practices can affect the relevance of existing evidence.

Research takeaway
Always evaluate the temporal relevance of a finding before generalizing it.

Definitions Matter

Variables that appear similar may capture different underlying phenomena.

Delayed school entry, statutory eligibility differences, and grade repetition can produce similar observed age differences while representing distinct mechanisms.

Research takeaway
Establish conceptual and measurement comparability before combining evidence.

Measurement Matters

Survey results may differ because of variations in respondent characteristics, measurement scales, and the constructs being assessed.

A reported stress score is not independently meaningful without understanding how stress was defined and measured.

Research takeaway
Interpret statistics within their methodological context rather than treating numerical differences as definitive conclusions.

Learning to ask better questions.

Studying Commerce and Computer Science has taught me to approach problems through both economic reasoning and computational analysis. Academic research is adding another dimension to that perspective: understanding the quality of the evidence behind a conclusion.

Through my research experience, I am learning to examine how data is collected, how variables are defined, and whether findings remain meaningful when applied to different populations or historical contexts.

Rather than treating a published statistic as a definitive answer, I have become more attentive to the assumptions, methodological choices, and limitations that determine what that statistic can actually tell us.

This experience continues to shape my broader interest in reliable data-driven decision-making, particularly when information is incomplete, assumptions are uncertain, and real-world conditions are complex.

Finding evidence is only the beginning. Understanding what that evidence can—and cannot—support is what makes research meaningful.
